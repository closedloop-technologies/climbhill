# Don't Drop the BATON

## Why this matters to ClimbHill

BATON shows that a stage can succeed locally while still leaving the system in a state that makes the next stage fail. Success flags are not enough; handoffs have contracts.

## What the paper establishes

BATON addresses long-horizon manipulation by making subtasks the unit of exploration and by explicitly modeling transitions between them. Instead of learning a whole long task through costly end-to-end exploration, it learns shorter subtask solutions and composes them. It also adds transition-aware memory: a verifier governs whether a skill may be invoked, handoff logic restores a usable entry state for the successor, and lookahead selects strategies whose resulting states are compatible with later stages. The key lesson is that correctness includes the state inherited by the next component.

## ClimbHill design consequences

For actions that participate in a larger workflow, capture:

- entry preconditions;
- expected exit state;
- successor postconditions;
- evidence that the handoff is valid;
- residual side effects that may constrain the next action.

A successful action should not automatically imply a valid transition.

## Design question

Should ClimbHill Evaluations be able to distinguish `action_success` from `handoff_valid`, so Continuation Analysis can detect a successful local Attempt that leaves the next authorized action impossible or unsafe?
